# Landing Page & Conversion Route

Use this route when one offer, one audience, and one primary action define the page: campaign destinations, product launches, waitlists, demo requests, trials, downloads, pricing pages, and focused signup pages.

A homepage may serve several intents; a landing page should not.

## Conversion contract

Establish:

- exact offer
- primary audience
- traffic source and what visitors already know
- desired action and what counts as conversion
- main motivation
- top objections and perceived risk
- available proof
- constraints such as price, eligibility, geography, timing, or compliance

If proof is missing, expose the gap. Do not fabricate logos, testimonials, metrics, reviews, or “trusted by” claims.

## Build the argument before the layout

A strong page normally needs these jobs, but the order and visual form depend on awareness and complexity:

1. orient the visitor
2. state a specific outcome or promise
3. make the product or offer concrete
4. establish relevance and stakes
5. explain the mechanism or experience
6. show benefits in observable terms
7. provide evidence
8. resolve objections and risk
9. make the next step obvious

High-intent visitors may need a short path. New or skeptical visitors may need a longer argument. Do not pad a simple offer into twelve sections.

## Hero test

Within a few seconds, the first viewport should answer:

- What is this?
- Who is it for?
- What useful outcome does it provide?
- What should I do next?
- Why should I trust the claim enough to continue?

Use one primary CTA. A secondary action is valid only when it represents a genuinely different commitment, such as watching a demo before starting a trial.

The hero visual must help explain the offer: real product capture, outcome, comparison, demonstration, or purposeful art direction. Decorative geometry is not product evidence.

## Message and proof

- Match the promise and language of the traffic source.
- Lead with outcomes, then explain capabilities.
- Prefer concrete mechanisms and constraints over superlatives.
- Place proof near the claim it supports.
- Use objection handling where resistance occurs, not automatically at the bottom.
- Explain price, cancellation, eligibility, privacy, setup effort, or switching cost when they influence the decision.
- Keep CTA labels consistent for the same action.

## Form and action design

Request only information needed at that stage. Explain sensitive or unusual fields. Preserve labels, autocomplete, paste, validation recovery, and keyboard behavior. Show progress when commitment spans multiple steps.

After submission, confirm what happened, what happens next, and how long the user should expect only when that timing is known.

## Experimentation

Create variants only around a named hypothesis, such as:

- product-first versus outcome-first hero
- short versus educational narrative
- self-serve versus sales-assisted CTA
- proof type or placement
- pricing framing

Keep unrelated variables constant. A palette swap is not a conversion experiment.

## Delivery checks

- offer and audience are unambiguous
- primary CTA has one meaning throughout
- every major claim has evidence, explanation, or an honest limitation
- page length reflects visitor awareness and offer complexity
- first viewport works at representative mobile and desktop sizes
- real content fits without clipping or awkward wrapping
- form states and error recovery work
- analytics events map to the funnel without collecting unnecessary data
- indexing and metadata match the campaign's intended lifetime and search value
- no generic sections exist solely because landing pages usually have them
