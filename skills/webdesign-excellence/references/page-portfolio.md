# Page route: portfolios, studios, and case studies

**Load when:** the purpose is to demonstrate work, judgment, or a creative practice.

## Lead with work rather than interface theatrics

Determine who is evaluating the work and what evidence they need. A hiring manager,
a client seeking a studio, and an art audience have different questions. Make the
practice, role, and strongest work understandable quickly. Do not make a custom
cursor or an elaborate menu the primary demonstration of capability.

## Curate a sequence

Choose a few representative projects and explain why they belong. Use different
image scales only where editorial importance differs. Keep project titles, roles,
dates, and categories consistent so the reader can scan. An equal grid is appropriate
for comparable work; a staggered layout is useful only when it reinforces hierarchy.

Use actual project assets with rights and permission. Clearly distinguish concept
work from commissioned work and personal contribution from team output. Do not
invent measurable impact, clients, awards, or outcomes.

## Case-study composition

Give context, constraints, contribution, the key decisions, and evidence of the result.
This need not become an exhaustive process diary. Show the artifact at a useful scale,
then details that explain a decision. Avoid dumping twenty screenshots without
commentary or presenting process diagrams as a substitute for finished work.

A strong case study should still work as a readable document with motion disabled.
Use sticky context, image treatment, or a short stack only when it improves the
sequence. Keep the next project and contact route easy to find. Describe what an
inquiry should include and what channel actually works.

## Acceptance

Can a reader distinguish what the person or studio actually did? Is the work readable
on a phone? Do hover thumbnails have an equivalent ordinary link and label? Are
all projects reachable by keyboard? Does the story work without the custom effect?

Check full-page rhythm as well as a hero crop. The most impressive visual should
usually belong to the work itself, not the frame around the portfolio.
